FR24 LADD Unblocker

A browser extension for Flightradar24. It names the flights FR24 hides or shows as "N/A", adds their route, owner and history, plays their flights back in FR24's own player, fills in the figures FR24 keeps behind a padlock and unlocks its locked weather layers and map options, all from public ADS-B and other free public data.

Features

FR24, with the blanks filled in

Everything is written into FR24's own data before FR24 draws it, so hidden aircraft look like ordinary traffic, and FR24's own buttons, tables and player do the rest.

Every blocked flight, named

Anonymous FR24 flights are matched to the transponder they really are. The shared monitor watches FR24's whole map, so flights over oceans, deserts and on the ground are named before you even look.

Where it's going

The take-off airport comes from the track. The destination is predicted by an explainable engine, with a probability and the reasons. It is only "Confirmed" when the aircraft is lined up on a runway. Flights FR24 shows without a destination get one too, on boards and in Most tracked.

Their flights, logged

Each blocked aircraft's departures and arrivals are kept for good, with the runway used. They appear in FR24's own "Recent flights", its aircraft pages, search and boards. Older flights are rebuilt under FR24's week too, for any aircraft, as far back as the public record goes.

Played back in FR24's own player

Press Play on any flight of a log, blocked or older than FR24's 7 days, and it plays at the top of the page in FR24's own player: map, slider, figures and graph. KML and CSV sit beside Play, as on FR24's own rows. How playback works.

Airports, filled in

Blocked flights on arrival and departure boards worldwide, without false "same-airport" flights from taxiing aircraft, and the runway of every flown row. Movements per day, runway usage, every runway in full and the radio frequencies, where FR24 shows a padlock.

Live telemetry, no padlocks

The fields FR24 shows behind padlocks, filled from public ADS-B for any aircraft: vertical speed, squawk, GPS altitude, airspeeds, Mach, wind, temperature and airspace. Where no receiver reports a figure it is worked out from the ones that are. The server and the browser share the polling.

FR24's locked map options

The weather layers (cloud, precipitation, radar, winds, lightning, icing, turbulence, significant weather, AIRMETs and SIGMETs), aeronautical charts, ATC boundaries, oceanic tracks, category filters, up to 60 custom filters, unlimited bookmarks of every kind and the Last clicked flights widget. Built from free public sources, ready on the monitor.

Ages, owners and pictures

Every aircraft gets an age, and a serial number where the public records have one, from registers and databases. Owners come from public registers, names only. Photos come from JetPhotos and Planespotters, with the photographer credited.

One window for every setting

A button on FR24's bottom bar opens a window styled like FR24's own. It holds a switch for every feature, the update button and the data sources, so you keep what you want and leave the rest as FR24 has it.

  • Owners and operators from public registers (FAA, UK G-INFO, Isle of Man, Switzerland): names only, never addresses.
  • Photos from JetPhotos and Planespotters, credited.
  • FR24's adverts removed; background work waits for idle moments, so the map never stutters.
  • FR24's own routes and airports always win: the extension only fills what FR24 leaves empty.

Playback

Playback, the way FR24 does it

FR24 gives a free account seven days of playback, and plays a hidden flight without a name. The project rebuilds those flights from the positions that were really broadcast, then plays them in the place and the player FR24 uses for its own.

  1. Press Play

    On any row of an aircraft's log, in FR24's own table. Each rebuilt flight has FR24's own Play button, with KML and CSV beside it, exactly as on any other row.

  2. FR24's page asks for the flight

    Its own page script requests the flight's track. For a flight only the project knows about, that request is answered inside your browser from the track as kept: the monitor's copy of FR24's own positions, or the public ADS-B record of the day.

  3. FR24's player draws it

    The map, the track coloured by altitude, the slider, the figures and the speed and altitude graph appear at the top of the same page. The address gets # and the flight's id, so the link can be shared.

Every figure, no padlocks

GPS altitude, true and indicated airspeed, vertical speed and squawk are filled for the moment being played. Between two reports up to two minutes apart a figure is taken in proportion; otherwise it comes from the nearer report within a minute. On the ground, or where an aircraft sends none, it reads N/A, as on FR24.

Every way in leads there

The Playback buttons on the map panel, airport boards, search results and recent flights, and the panel's "playback" link, all open the aircraft's log page at that flight. A board's take-off time plays the flight that took off within three hours of it. A middle or Ctrl click opens it in a new tab.

KML and CSV, in FR24's own formats

The files are made from the same track, with FR24's own CSV columns (timestamp, UTC, callsign, position, altitude, speed, direction). A flight with no track anywhere gets FR24's own "not available" message instead of an empty file.

Where the track comes from

Every position the monitor got from FR24, kept exactly as sent, and the public record of the day (adsb.lol, with airplanes.live filling its holes). The fuller record is used and the other fills its gaps, thinned to about one point every eight seconds in the air, like FR24's own.

The honest limit. A flight needs a track. If no receiver heard it and FR24 has no positions of it, there is nothing to play, and FR24's own message says so. Where coverage had a hole, the track has a gap, as it does on FR24.

How it works

From a radio broadcast to FR24's screen

The shared monitor listens all the time and worldwide, which a browser tab cannot do. Your browser reacts instantly to what is on screen, which the server cannot. Together they turn public broadcasts into the blanks FR24 leaves.

  1. The aircraft broadcasts

    ADS-B Out sends identity, position, altitude and speed about twice a second, unencrypted, on 1090 MHz. It is required by law in most controlled airspace.

  2. Volunteers receive and publish

    Thousands of hobbyists' receivers hear it. Community networks (adsb.lol, adsb.fi, airplanes.live, OpenSky) publish it openly, with the day's full traces.

  3. FR24 draws some flights blank

    For a hidden or "N/A" flight FR24 sends a position, altitude, speed, track and usually a type, but no callsign or registration.

  4. The monitor matches it

    It scores every aircraft heard nearby against that blank flight and names it only when one is clearly the best match. It then works out the route, the same answer for everyone.

  5. Your browser writes it in

    The extension adds the name, route, figures and history to FR24's own data in your tab, before FR24 draws it. Nothing is overlaid.

  6. FR24 draws ordinary traffic

    FR24's own panel, boards, search and player show it as if it had been there all along.

The monitor

A small always-on server. Every copy of the script uses the shared public one by default.

  • It follows adsb.lol's worldwide lists of blocked, military and privacy-address aircraft (every 10 seconds to 2 minutes), and every known blocked aircraft by address.
  • It reads FR24's whole map every two minutes and names every flight FR24 shows without a callsign, from public ADS-B.
  • It works out every flight's route, all the time, so everyone sees the same answer, and checks each one against where the flight really landed.
  • It logs every blocked aircraft's take-offs and landings for good, including flights only FR24 can see, with the runway used. A world log keeps every aircraft's flights for each day of the past year.
  • It keeps FR24's own positions as they came for 730 days, so old flights can be played back.
  • It builds the weather layers, and keeps every aircraft's registration, type, serial number, age and owner, about 760,000 of them.
  • It serves runway and frequency data for every airport.

The browser

The extension or userscript, inside the FR24 tab.

  • It takes the monitor's whole names table the moment FR24 opens, so FR24's very first frame of any blocked flight is drawn named.
  • It sends what it sees of the flights on screen to the monitor, which works out every route, the same for everyone, and shows its answer.
  • It writes everything into FR24's own data before FR24 draws it, and answers FR24's own playback requests for flights only the project knows about.
  • It runs only in idle moments, and stands aside while you drag or zoom the map.
  • If the monitor is unreachable, it identifies flights from public ADS-B on its own.
How a blocked flight gets its name

FR24 reports a hidden flight with its position, altitude, speed, track and usually its type. The monitor and the browser match it against every aircraft the public networks heard around then:

  • Projection. Each candidate is carried forward to the moment of FR24's report by dead reckoning on its own speed and track.
  • Scoring. Candidates are scored on distance, altitude, track and speed differences, how the aircraft types relate (same, same family, unknown; a different type rejects it), registration agreement and list flags.
  • A clear winner only. A match is accepted only when the second-best candidate scores at least 2.5 worse or is more than 8 km away. The distance allowed grows with speed, since two sources' clocks disagree by a few seconds and a jet covers kilometres in that time.
  • Out of coverage. Over oceans and deserts, FR24's own trail of the flight is compared with candidates carried forward from where they were last heard, the day's traces of blocked aircraft of that type and adsb.lol's heatmap. One unambiguous agreement is required.
  • Parked aircraft. On an apron no network hears, the only blocked aircraft of that type whose last flight ended at that airport and has not flown since.
  • Kept honest. Every name keeps being checked against new reports, and is withdrawn on overwhelming disagreement: far apart, repeatedly, for more than three minutes.
How a flight log is built

An aircraft's public track is split into flights at its landings. The engine counts ground reports, slow gaps, final approaches and "lost low beside a field". A hole in coverage counts as a landing only when the aircraft cannot have flown straight through it, and legs split by holes are joined again.

  • Airports. Take-offs and landings are placed at airports, at fields FR24 does not list (from OurAirports) and at learned private landing sites for helicopters. Each end carries a confidence: on the ground, low, or near.
  • Runways. The runway is the end whose extended centreline the aircraft was on, going that way: down the final approach or climbing out from the threshold. The answer is one clear runway or none.
  • The monitor first. For a blocked aircraft the days the monitor has rebuilt are taken as they are, and the rest come from adsb.lol's day traces, with airplanes.live filling in older days.
  • FR24's own track first. Where FR24 had a flight and no public receiver did, its positions go in, so take-offs and landings only FR24 saw are in the log.
  • One engine, two places. The browser and the monitor run the same engine file, so the map, boards and logs agree. A test checks that a log from the monitor is identical to the one the browser builds itself.
  • Empty days are claimed carefully. The monitor says "no flights that day" only when a list watched the aircraft without a gap all day and never heard it airborne. If in doubt, the browser reads the day itself.
How the padlocked figures are filled

FR24 does not send the figures it padlocks for an aircraft it hides, so they cannot be "unlocked" from FR24. They are filled from other public sources instead:

  • From the aircraft itself. Vertical speed, squawk, GPS altitude, indicated and true airspeed, Mach, wind and temperature come from the newest public ADS-B report of that aircraft. The monitor keeps the latest report of every aircraft it hears and shares it.
  • Where no receiver reports it. Many aircraft do not send airspeeds or wind. Over most of the United States and the oceans the monitor works them out from NOAA's GFS weather model: true and indicated airspeed, Mach, wind and temperature. Vertical speed is worked out from the altitude changes. The squawk is the code last heard in the air since take-off. Airspace comes from the FIR and UIR boundaries.
  • Weather layers. Cloud, precipitation, radar, winds, lightning, icing, turbulence and significant-weather layers come from free public sources (EUMETSAT, NASA, RainViewer, Iowa State, the Aviation Weather Center, Open-Meteo, Blitzortung), built into tiles on the monitor.
How the destination is worked out

The engine scores every plausible airport, plus the hypothesis "a local flight returning home", on transparent, weighted evidence:

  • The aircraft's own history: routes flown in each direction, what usually follows what, home base, overnight stops, weekdays and hours, cruise levels and the paths it flew.
  • The flight so far: distance and heading against each great circle, descent profile, selected altitude, squawk and callsign.
  • Feasibility: range, and runway length, width, surface and lighting for this aircraft type.
  • Everyone else's habits: learned from 63 days of every aircraft adsb.lol saw, plus every blocked aircraft's flights as the monitor logs them, scored on aircraft held out of training.
  • Live conditions: weather (METAR/TAF) and airport closures (NOTAMs, with an optional FAA key).
  • The aircraft's own datalink: many business jets send their route over ACARS. adsb.lol publishes what its receivers hear as open data, about an hour behind. Where a message names this flight's origin, its destination is taken as filed: on 21 October that was the airport landed at 362 times in 367.
  • Filed plans: a flight FR24's own boards list gives its plan, and an owner's other aircraft help predict where each other are going.

The result is a probability per airport, with the reasons in the tooltip. "Confirmed" is reserved for an aircraft established on a runway's final approach, which is right 99.8% of the time on held-out data, and otherwise the figure never goes above 99%. Predictions are predictions, and are sometimes wrong. Full details: architecture.

How it stays out of the way, and out of trouble
  • Idle slices. Background work runs 8 ms at a time, only when the browser is idle, and not at all while you drag or zoom. Heavy jobs run on the monitor. Main-thread stalls are measured on every change to the map path.
  • Gentle with free networks. One monitor asks once instead of every viewer asking. It sends at most 20 requests a minute to adsb.lol, one at a time and at least two seconds apart, and pauses on any refusal. Browsers stop polling lists once the monitor answers.
  • A gentle map scan. One request every 0.4 seconds, one round every two minutes. A refusal pauses it for ten minutes, doubling up to two hours, and the browsers go back to their own scan.
  • Fail soft. The page interceptor rewrites only what it recognises and passes everything else through untouched, so an FR24 change makes a feature stop, not the page break. A failing source never blocks the others, and every dependency has a defined fallback.
  • Free to run. The monitor uses only Node.js's standard library, with nothing to audit or update, and fits a free 1 GB cloud VM.
  • Native, not overlaid. Names and figures are written into FR24's own data, so FR24's own interface shows them.
  • FR24 wins. Its filed routes and airports always stand; the extension only fills what FR24 leaves empty.
  • Honest predictions. Weighted evidence with reasons you can read, never a black box, and "Confirmed" only on a runway's final approach.
  • Tested on real data. Fixtures are recorded traces and FR24 answers from real flights, and FR24's own front-end is run in Chromium to test changes.

Legal

Built on public data, inside the law

The project's legal position follows from a few facts about how it works: it receives no radio, it uses only data others have already published, it changes only what your own browser shows, and it picks no lock. Here is the reasoning, one point at a time.

Not legal advice. This is the author's analysis of a personal project, written to explain its design. Laws differ between countries and change. If your situation matters (commercial use, publishing the data, a letter from someone's lawyer), ask a lawyer qualified where you live. The full reasoning is in the legal notes.

ADS-B is a public broadcast

Aircraft with ADS-B Out broadcast their identity, position, altitude and speed about twice a second, unencrypted, on 1090 MHz, to anyone in range. That is the point of it: air traffic control, other aircraft and ground stations all rely on it being receivable. In most controlled airspace the broadcast is required by law. Tens of thousands of hobbyists receive it, many feeding Flightradar24 itself.

US: 14 CFR §91.225 and §91.227 · EU: Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 1207/2011 · UK: its retained version.

Blocking is an agreement, not a law of the sky

Some owners ask the FAA to limit what it passes to industry about their aircraft. A company that takes the FAA's data agrees to honour that for that data. It is a condition of a data feed. It does not make an aircraft's own broadcast secret, and cannot: the broadcast is required and unencrypted. It binds neither community receivers nor anyone who takes no FAA data.

The FAA recognises the gap. It created temporary privacy addresses, not linked to the owner in the public register, for owners who want to limit identification from the broadcast itself. Flightradar24 hides these aircraft because of its own arrangements: a business decision that binds Flightradar24, not a legal status that binds everyone else. The community networks mark these aircraft with a flag and still publish their positions, under their own terms.

The project receives no radio

It operates no receiver. It reads positions that community networks have already received and published openly, under their own terms. Rules on who may receive radio messages are aimed at whoever operates a receiver, and this project operates none.

For example, the UK's Wireless Telegraphy Act 2006, s.48.

Facts are not owned

Aircraft positions, times, routes and runway lengths are facts, and copyright protects expression, not facts. The project copies none of Flightradar24's code, design, text or images: its tables, texts and layouts are its own.

Feist Publications v. Rural Telephone Service, 499 U.S. 340 (1991). The UK and EU reach the same result by requiring originality.

No lock is picked

No login, no paywall, no credentials and no paid account: the project uses Flightradar24's public pages and the same unauthenticated requests any visitor's browser makes. Nothing is decrypted. The padlocked figures of a hidden aircraft are not unlocked from Flightradar24 at all, because Flightradar24 never sends them. They are filled from other public sources.

Van Buren v. United States, 593 U.S. 374 (2021); hiQ Labs v. LinkedIn, 31 F.4th 1180 (9th Cir. 2022). Anti-circumvention law protects effective technological measures (17 U.S.C. §1201; Directive 2001/29/EC, Art. 6); the UK's Computer Misuse Act 1990, s.1, turns on unauthorised access.

Your browser, your screen

How a page appears on your own screen is your choice. That is the principle behind ad blockers, reader modes, translation and accessibility tools. Germany's Federal Court of Justice upheld it against a publisher: a tool that helps the user decide what their browser shows is not an unlawful obstruction. This project removes adverts and adds information in the same way, in the browser of the person who installed it, and nowhere else.

BGH, 19 April 2018, I ZR 154/16 (Werbeblocker II).

Open licences, kept

adsb.lol publishes under the Open Database License, and is credited wherever its data appears. Because the shared monitor is publicly used, it offers the flights it logs under the same licence, a day at a time. Other networks' rate limits are respected, approval-gated APIs are not used, a refusal pauses a source instead of being worked around, and API keys are always the user's own. OurAirports is public domain, OpenStreetMap is credited, and photographs are shown with credit to their photographers.

ODbL 1.0, §4.4 (share-alike). Every source and its terms: data sources.

Personal data, handled narrowly

Owners are looked up in public registers, which publish them so that anyone can see who owns an aircraft. The project reads names only: street addresses are never fetched, stored or shown. In your own browser, personal use falls within the household exemption. The shared monitor is a service, so it names an owner only when the register shows an organisation (a company, trust, bank or club), and withholds the name and the town of a private individual. Positions are not kept against people.

UK and EU GDPR: Article 2(2)(c), the household exemption; Article 6(1)(f), legitimate interests.

Its name, used fairly

"Flightradar24" is used only to say which website the tool works with, which trademark law allows. No logos or get-up are used, nothing implies affiliation, and the footer says plainly that the project is independent of Flightradar24 AB, the FAA and every data provider named here.

Where the limits are. The household exemption ends where personal use ends: using this data to follow, locate or contact a person is never acceptable with this project. What the shared monitor gives your browser is for the tool's own use. Running another public monitor, or publishing what one serves, brings in the ODbL's share-alike terms and the data-protection duties of a service, and the licence requires the author's permission first. Predicted destinations are predictions, identifications can be wrong, and none of it is for navigation, safety, security or any decision with consequences.

Flightradar24

What Flightradar24 can and can't do about it

Flightradar24 AB is a Swedish company with real rights over its own website, code and database. None of them reaches a public radio broadcast, or what your own browser does with a page. This is where the line falls.

×What it can't do

  • Make ADS-B private, or claim ownership of what aircraft transmit.
  • Control the community networks, which receive and publish independently, under their own licences.
  • Extend an owner's request to the FAA to anyone who takes no FAA data.
  • Control what your browser displays, or stop you installing an extension.
  • Claim copyright in aircraft positions, routes, runway lengths or registry facts.

✓What it can do, and what happens

  • Change its website. A feature may stop until the project is adapted. The extension is built to fail soft, rewriting only what it recognises, and updates follow. The in-page banner tells you when one is out.
  • Refuse requests from an address, such as the monitor's server. The monitor backs off on any refusal, and your browser carries on reading its own map, as it did before the monitor existed.
  • End its service to an account under its own terms. Terms of use are a contract with the user, and the usual remedy for breaching them is ending that service, not control over what a browser displays.

Why it is structurally hard to stop

The data isn't Flightradar24's to recall

Names, routes and figures come from broadcasts and from networks that publish them under their own licences. Flightradar24 cannot withdraw them, because it never held them exclusively.

The change happens on your device

The page is rewritten in your own browser, after Flightradar24 has sent it, for the person who chose to install the extension. That is the same place an ad blocker or translator works.

Nothing is taken that isn't already sent

The extension reads what Flightradar24 sends every visitor's browser for display. The padlocked figures of a hidden aircraft are never sent, so there is nothing to extract: they come from elsewhere.

It makes you use Flightradar24 more

Every name it finds is shown on Flightradar24's own map, in its own pages, with its own player. It is not a substitute for the service, and it only fills what Flightradar24 leaves empty.

Where the project is least protected, said plainly. The monitor reads Flightradar24's map every two minutes, the same answer every visitor's browser is sent, so that it can name flights from public ADS-B. Flightradar24's terms are unlikely to welcome automated reading of its feed. In the EU and UK the question to weigh is the database right, which protects a maker's investment against a substitute service and not against a tool that sends its users back to the original (CV-Online Latvia v Melons, C-762/19, 2021). Courts are cautious about website terms nobody saw or accepted (Specht v. Netscape, 306 F.3d 17; Nguyen v. Barnes & Noble, 763 F.3d 1171), although EU law lets a site restrict use of a database by contract (Ryanair v PR Aviation, C-30/14).

So the scan is gentle by design, stops at any refusal, never borrows your Flightradar24 identity, and can be switched off. The project is built to work without it, and the browsers do their own scanning if it stops.

Privacy

Nothing about you is the product

There are no accounts, cookies, analytics or advertising: not in the extension and not on this site. What the shared monitor needs to answer your map is listed below, and you can turn it off or use your own.

Stays in your browser

A cache in the browser's own storage so it does not ask twice: aircraft identities and owners, flight logs, learned routes, airport boards and your settings. Any API key you enter yourself is stored there too and sent only to the service it belongs to. Removing the extension, or clearing the site's data, deletes it.

What the shared monitor receives

The area your map shows, the aircraft and boards you open, the nameless flights your page shows and the names your browser found for them, owners it looked up in public registers, and the public ADS-B reports it fetched. Most of it is kept without your address, for minutes or days. Your IP address appears in the web server's access log for up to 14 days, and in a one-minute rate limit.

What it never receives

Your Flightradar24 account, cookies, password or e-mail address, anything typed into a page, or Flightradar24's device id for your browser. That id goes only to a monitor of your own, and never an authorisation or a cookie.

Counting installs, and the off switch

So the project knows roughly how many people use it, each install says hello at most once every 12 hours: a random number made by your browser, the version, whether it is the extension or a userscript, the userscript manager, and the browser and system by name only. Nothing about flights, the map or your account. Switch it off with "Count this install (anonymous)" in the LADD Unblock window.

Get it

Install

Use one or the other, not both at once. If you switch to the extension, turn the userscript off in your userscript manager.

For Google Chrome, Microsoft Edge and Brave (and other Chromium browsers, version 111 or later).

  1. Download

    Get the extension zip from the latest release and unzip it to a folder you'll keep.

    Before the first release is tagged, take it from the repository instead.

  2. Load unpacked

    Open chrome://extensions, switch on Developer mode, click Load unpacked and choose the unzipped folder.

    Edge: edge://extensions · Brave: brave://extensions

  3. Open Flightradar24

    Open flightradar24.com. That's it: blocked flights appear named, with nothing else to set up.

Firefox on the desktop, version 140 or later, has its own build.

  1. Get the Firefox build

    Download the Firefox zip from the latest release. It is the file sent to Firefox Add-ons.

  2. Try it before it is listed

    Open about:debugging#/runtime/this-firefox, click Load Temporary Add-on… and choose the zip. Firefox removes a temporary add-on when it restarts.

  3. Or use the userscript

  1. Get a manager

Every copy of the script connects to the shared public monitor by default, so it knows every blocked flight worldwide from the moment FR24 opens, along with each aircraft's history. There is nothing to set up.

To turn it off, or point it at your own monitor, open any aircraft's panel, click sources on the strip under the photo, and use the Monitor row, or use the Data sources section of the LADD Unblock window. An address and secret you enter there stay in your browser. Turned off, your browser identifies what it sees on its own.

Your own monitor is a single Node.js program (version 18 or later) with no dependencies. A free Oracle Cloud VM with 1 GB of RAM is enough.

node monitor/monitor.cjs        # try it locally: listens on 127.0.0.1:47524
bash monitor/vm-setup.sh        # on a server: systemd service, compressed HTTPS via Caddy
bash monitor/deploy.sh          # later: update, verify, roll back automatically on failure

Configuration, every endpoint and day-to-day operation: docs/monitor.md. A monitor you make public must offer its derived flights under the ODbL; the legal notes explain why.

Every setting is in one place: click LADD Unblock on FR24's bottom bar, next to Playback (or the version number on the strip under an aircraft's photo). It opens a window like FR24's own Widgets and Weather windows, with:

  • Enable LADD Unblock, to switch the whole thing off and leave FR24 as it is (applies after a reload).
  • A switch for each feature: the aircraft panel details, airport boards, search, aircraft and flight pages, take-off and landing alerts, hiding ads and upgrade prompts, and FR24's locked map options (weather layers, charts, ATC boundaries, oceanic tracks, aircraft category filters).
  • Updates: the version you have, the latest one, and an Update button.
  • Data sources: which feeds are answering, the monitor, and your optional ADS-B Exchange and FAA NOTAM keys.
  • Count this install (anonymous): on by default, and off with one click.

The switches are kept in your browser only.

When a new version is out, a banner at the top of Flightradar24's map says so. Click Update on it, or in the LADD Unblock window.

  1. Extension

    A tab opens that puts the new version into the extension's folder for you. It downloads the files from the release, checks each one against the checksum the release lists, replaces the old ones and deletes those the new version no longer has. Then click Refresh page.

    The first time, Chrome asks you to choose the folder you unzipped the extension into and to allow it to be edited. Where a browser does not allow that (Brave, for one), the page links the new zip instead: unzip it over the old folder and click Reload extension.

  2. Userscript

    Open the new userscript file again. Tampermonkey and Violentmonkey also check for updates on their own.

  3. What's new

FAQ

Questions, answered straight

Does it need a Flightradar24 account or subscription?

No. It works with a free account or none at all. It uses no paid feature and no one's login: the figures, layers and files come from free public sources.

Is this legal?

It is built to be. Aircraft broadcast ADS-B openly, by law. The project uses only data that community networks and public registers already publish. It changes only what your own browser shows you, and it breaks no lock. The reasoning, with its authorities and its limits, is in the legal section and in the legal notes. It is not legal advice.

Can Flightradar24 stop it?

It cannot make a public broadcast private, control the community networks the names come from, or control what your own browser displays. It can change its website, refuse requests from an address, or end service to an account under its own terms. If it changes its site, a feature may stop until the project is adapted; the extension fails soft and updates follow. The details are here.

Is it against Flightradar24's terms?

Terms of use are a contract between Flightradar24 and its users. Courts are cautious about terms a visitor never saw or accepted, and a breach is usually answered by ending the service to that account, not by control over your browser. The part of the project Flightradar24's terms are least likely to welcome is the monitor's automated reading of its map. That is optional, gentle, stops at any refusal, and the project works without it. If it matters to you, read the section above and decide for yourself.

Does it unlock Flightradar24's paid features?

No. It does not take anything from Flightradar24's servers that is not already sent to every visitor. The padlocked figures of a hidden aircraft are never sent, so they are filled from other public sources, and the weather layers, charts and downloads are made from free public data. Nothing is decrypted and no one's login is used.

Why does a destination say "Predicted 64%"?

Blocked flights have no public flight plan. The destination is worked out from the evidence and shown with its probability. It turns "Confirmed" only on final approach to a runway, which is right 99.8% of the time on held-out data. The tooltip lists the reasons.

How accurate is it?

Names are accepted only when one aircraft is clearly the best match, and are withdrawn on overwhelming disagreement. Predicted destinations are predictions and are sometimes wrong, which is why they carry a probability. Don't use any of it for navigation, safety, security or any decision with consequences.

What does the shared monitor learn about me?

Only what it needs to answer your map: the area you view, the aircraft and boards you open, and the names and public ADS-B reports your browser found. It never receives your Flightradar24 account, cookies, password or anything you type. The full list, and how long each part is kept, is in the privacy section and the privacy notice.

Can I use it to find out who owns an aircraft, or to follow someone?

It is for personal interest, and must not be used to track, follow, locate or contact anyone. Owners come from public registers, names only and never addresses, and the shared monitor names an owner only when the register shows an organisation.

Will it slow Flightradar24 down?

No. Background work runs in short slices while the browser is idle, and pauses while you drag or zoom the map. Heavy work runs on the monitor, and main-thread stalls are measured on every change to the map path.

Do I need to run a monitor?

No. Every copy connects to the shared public monitor by default. You can turn it off, or use your own instead, in the sources menu or the LADD Unblock window.

What if the monitor is down?

The extension carries on by itself, asking adsb.lol and adsb.fi directly. It keeps the last names it was given for six hours.

What happens when Flightradar24 changes its site?

The extension only rewrites data it recognises and passes everything else through untouched, so a change makes a feature stop rather than the page break. Changes are tested against Flightradar24's own front-end running in Chromium, and when something needs adapting an update follows, announced by the banner on the map.

Which browsers does it work in? Does it work on a phone?

Chrome, Edge and Brave (and other Chromium browsers, version 111 or later), Firefox 140 or later, and any browser with a userscript manager. It is made for desktop browsers and has not been tested on phones.

How do I report a problem, or a security issue?

Open an issue on GitHub. How to report a security problem, and how secrets and keys are handled, is in SECURITY.md.
